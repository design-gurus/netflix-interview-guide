# Common Mistakes in the Netflix Interview

Most failed Netflix loops are reported to fail on judgment, independence, or culture, not on algorithms. The ten mistakes below are specific to this loop.

## The ten mistakes

| # | Mistake | Round |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Not reading the culture memo | Every round |
| 2 | Praising the memo without one honest disagreement | Culture |
| 3 | Removing the uncomfortable part from a story | Culture |
| 4 | Answering "Why Netflix?" with praise for the product | Recruiter and manager screens |
| 5 | Preparing only algorithm puzzles | Coding |
| 6 | Stopping at the first working version | Coding |
| 7 | Waiting for the interviewer to lead the design | System design |
| 8 | Designing the whole streaming service without scoping | System design |
| 9 | Presenting a design with no trade-offs and no failure behavior | System design |
| 10 | Describing past projects without numbers | Manager screen and onsite loop |

## Culture and motivation

**1. Not reading the culture memo.** Interviewers are reported to notice within one minute who has read it. Read it before any other preparation.

**2. Praising the memo without one honest disagreement.** Interviewers ask what you disagree with. A specific and respectful disagreement is stronger than fluent praise, because candor is one of the values under test.

**3. Removing the uncomfortable part from a story.** Interviewers are reported to detect stories that were cleaned up. Keep the moment when your feedback was received badly, then describe what happened next.

**4. Answering "Why Netflix?" with praise for the product.** This is reported as the most common mistake in the first calls. Build the answer from your own work and from one part of the memo.

## Coding

**5. Preparing only algorithm puzzles.** Many teams prefer a small real task, such as a rate limiter or a parser. A rate limiter is a component that caps how many requests one user can send in a period. Practice building small components in a real editor, and keep the basics fast.

**6. Stopping at the first working version.** Follow-up questions arrive after the code runs. Expect questions about input that does not fit in memory and about two threads writing at once. Answer aloud and name what each choice costs.

## System design

**7. Waiting for the interviewer to lead the design.** The round is reported to be open-ended. Netflix expects engineers to work with little direction, so the candidate is expected to drive. State your plan in the first two minutes and ask your own clarifying questions.

**8. Designing the whole streaming service without scoping.** Nobody can design all of Netflix in 45 minutes. State what you will cover first, what you will cut, and why. Recommendations and billing are common cuts when the question is about playback.

**9. Presenting a design with no trade-offs and no failure behavior.** Interviewers ask what breaks first at ten times the traffic and what a viewer sees when a region fails. Raise both points before you are asked.

## Past work and level

**10. Describing past projects without numbers.** "We improved performance" invites a follow-up question that needs a measurement. Prepare two or three projects to a depth of four follow-up questions, with the numbers, the constraints, and what you would change now.

A related problem affects mid-level candidates. They are reported to pass the technical rounds and still receive no offer, because their stories show assigned work and not independent decisions. Choose stories in which you defined the scope yourself. The [levels](levels.md) page explains the difference between L4 and L5.
